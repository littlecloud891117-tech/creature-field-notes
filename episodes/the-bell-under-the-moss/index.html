The Bell Under the Moss

A watershed keeper must choose between using a rare creature and earning its trust before a flood arrives.

The old flood bell had not rung in fourteen years. Ila heard it on a morning without wind.

One low note crossed the reed beds and entered the floorboards of the keeper's house. Cups trembled in their hooks. Dust fell from the map shelf. By the time Ila reached the bell tower, the sound had stopped.

Moss covered the bronze bell from lip to crown. Something small moved beneath it.

Ila knelt and lifted a green curtain of moss. A creature stared back with six dark eyes. It was no larger than a loaf of bread. Pale plates overlapped across its round back, and each plate held a thin gold line. Its four feet clung to the tower stone. A root, wound tight around one hind leg, held it below the bell.

Ila had seen a drawing of such a creature once. The old survey called it a knellback. The note beside the drawing said only: Do not strike the shell.

She reached for the root. The knellback pulled into itself.

Every sound vanished.

The reeds stopped their dry whisper. The tower ropes stopped creaking. Ila clapped once and felt her palms meet, but heard nothing. The silence lasted for three breaths. Then the creature's plates loosened, and the world returned all at once.

Below the tower, Keeper Senn called her name. He carried the morning river gauge in one hand. A red mark crossed its highest line.

"Storm water from the north," he said. "It will reach us tonight. We need the bell."

Ila showed him the creature and the trapped leg.

Senn looked at the silent bell, then at the black clouds above the ridge. "Move it. Carefully if you can. Quickly if you cannot."

The tower had one purpose. When the river climbed, its bell sent people to the high road. The village trusted that sound more than any keeper.

Ila brought a knife, oil, and a shallow wooden box. She cut the outer roots first. Each time the blade touched the tight coil, the knellback swallowed every sound around them. Ila could not hear the warning calls from the river crew. She could not hear Senn climb the ladder behind her.

"Put it in the box," he said when sound returned. "Free the leg after we ring."

Ila placed the box beside the creature. Its six eyes followed the shadow inside.

She imagined the lid closing. She imagined the bell hammer falling. The warning would reach every house. The creature would feel that blow through each pale plate.

Do not strike the shell.

The survey note was not a handling rule. It was an account of fear.

Ila slid the box away.

"No," she said. "We warn them by hand."

Senn stared at the rising river, then pulled the red signal cloth from his belt. "East path," he said. "I take west."

They ran from door to door while rain erased their footprints. Families climbed toward the high road. The river crossed the lower footbridge before sunset. Still, three reed cutters had not returned.

Ila went back to the tower alone.

Water circled its first step. The knellback remained under the bell. Ila sat in the cold water and rested her knife on the stone where the creature could see it. She did not touch the root again.

"Three people cannot hear us," she said. "I will not use you. But I need to free you."

She waited.

Rain struck the bell. The knellback opened one plate, then another. A gold line brightened across its back. It placed its trapped foot beside Ila's knife.

Ila cut the root.

The creature climbed onto her wrist. Its feet were colder than the flood. It pressed one shell plate against the tower wall.

The bell did not move.

Its note came from below.

Roots under the tower carried a deep vibration into the reed beds. Every stem bent, rose, and bent again. The sound traveled through water and mud instead of air. Far beyond the flooded bridge, three red lamps answered from the reeds.

The cutters had heard.

The knellback stepped from Ila's wrist. The gold line on the plate that had touched the wall was gone. Only a pale groove remained, and it did not brighten again. The creature disappeared beneath the moss before Ila could decide whether to thank it.

At dawn, the flood began to fall. A new gold line crossed the bell's green surface, as thin as the mark the knellback had lost. Ila did not clean it away.
